Differentials of reciprocal functions

What is the differential of \(\cos x\) ?

It's easy to see from the graph \(y=\cos x\) and the fact that it is a translation of the sin graph, that \[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\cos x=-\sin x\]

but we can also use the definition of a differential:

\begin{align*} f(x)&=\cos x\\[12pt] f'(x)&=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\\[12pt] &=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\cos(x+h)-\cos x}{h}\\[12pt] &=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\cos x\cos h-\sin x\sin h-\cos x}{h}\\[12pt] &=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\cos x\cos h-\sin x\sin h-\cos x}{h}\\[12pt] &=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{-\cos x(1-\cos h)-\sin x\sin h}{h}\\[12pt] &=-\cos x\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{(1-\cos h)}{h}-\sin x\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\sin h}{h}\\[12pt] &=-\sin x \end{align*}
Use the quotient rule to find the differential of \(\tan x\).
\begin{align*} f(x)&=\sec x=\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\\[12pt] f'(x)&=\frac{\cos^2 x+\sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x}\\[12pt] &=\frac{1}{\cos^2 x}=\sec^2 x\\[12pt] \end{align*}
Use the quotient rule to find the differential of \(\sec x\).
\begin{align*} f(x)&=\sec x=\frac{1}{\cos x}\\[12pt] f'(x)&=\frac{\sin x}{\cos^2 x}=\sec x\tan x\\[12pt] \end{align*}

or if you prefer to differentiate everything using only the essential definition of a differential:

\begin{align*} f(x)&=\sec x\\[12pt] f'(x)&=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\\[12pt] &=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\sec(x+h)-\sec x}{h}\\[12pt] &=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\frac{1}{\cos(x+h)}-\frac{1}{\cos x}}{h}\\[12pt] &=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\cos x-\cos(x+h)}{h\cos x\cos (x+h)}\\[12pt] &=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\cos x-\cos (x+h)}{h}\frac{1}{\cos x\cos (x+h)}\\[12pt] &=\sin x\frac{1}{\cos^2 x}\\[12pt] &=\frac{\sin x}{\cos^2 x}=\sec x\tan x\\ \end{align*}
Use the quotient rule to find the differential of \(\operatorname{cosec} x\).
\begin{align*} f(x)&=\operatorname{cosec} x=\frac{1}{\sin x}\\[12pt] f'(x)&=-\frac{\cos x}{\sin^2 x}=-\operatorname{cosec} x\cot x\\[12pt] \end{align*}
Use the quotient rule to find the differential of \(\cot x\).
\begin{align*} f(x)&=\cot x=\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\\[12pt] f'(x)&=\frac{-\sin^2 x-\cos^2 x}{\sin^2 x}\\[12pt] &=-\frac{1}{\sin^2 x}=-\operatorname{cosec}^2 x\\[12pt] \end{align*}

Well done!

You've now seen how the graphs of \(y=\sin x\), \(y=\cos x\), and \(y=\tan x\) arise directly from the unit circle. This connection is fundamental to everything that follows.
