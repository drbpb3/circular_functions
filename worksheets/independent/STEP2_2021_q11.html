Let's simplify the notation a bit.

First, let's number the seats so that each passenger \(i\) is assigned seat number \(i\), and

\[T_i=\text{the seat number of passenger }i\]

If \(T_1=1\), then every passenger will be in their assigned seat, so

\[\begin{align*} P\left(T_n=n|T_1=1\right)&=1\\[12pt] P\left(T_n=n\cap T_1=1\right)&=P\left(T_n=n|T_1=1\right)P\left(T_1=1\right)\\[12pt] &=\frac{1}{n} \end{align*}\]

If \(T_1=6\), then passengers 2 to 5 will be in their assigned seats, so we only need to consider passengers 6 to \(n\). To make things a little bit easier to grasp, let's take \(n=16\). Now we have 5 passengers in seats 2 to 6 and 11 passengers still to sit in seats 1 and 7 to 16.

\[\begin{align*} P\left(T_{16}=16|T_1=6\right)&=P(T_{11}=11)=P_{11}\\[12pt] P\left(T_n=n|T_1=6\right)&=P(T_{n-5}=n-5)=P_{n-5}\\[12pt] P\left(T_n=n\cap T_1=6\right)&=P\left(T_n=n|T_1=6\right)P\left(T_1=6\right)\\[12pt] &=\frac{1}{n}P_{n-5}\\[12pt] P\left(T_n=n\cap T_1=k\right)&=\frac{1}{n}P_{n-k+1}\\[12pt] \text{when }1 < k < n \end{align*}\]

And lastly

\[\begin{align*} P\left(T_n=n|T_1=n\right)&=0\\[12pt] P\left(T_n=n\cap T_1=n\right)&=0 \end{align*}\]

Now we can put things together:

\begin{align*} P_n&=\sum_1^n P(T_n=n\cap T_1=k)\\[12pt] &=\sum_1^n P(T_n=n|T_1=k)P(T_1=k)\\[12pt] &=\frac{1}{n}\sum_1^n P(T_n=n|T_1=k)\\[12pt] &=\frac{1}{n}\left(P(T_n=n|T_1=1)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_2^{n-1} P(T_n=n|T_1=k)+P(T_n=n|T_1=n)\right)\\[12pt] &=\frac{1}{n}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\sum_2^{n-1} P(T_n=n|T_1=k)+0\right)\\[12pt] &=\frac{1}{n}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\sum_2^{n-1} P_{n-k+1}\right)\\[12pt] \end{align*}

Now put \(r=n-k+1\) so that \(2\le r\le n-1\), and

\begin{align*} P_n&=\frac{1}{n}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=2}^{n-1} P_{r}\right) \end{align*}

We can see pretty easily that \(P_2=\frac{1}{2}\), and then the recurrence relation shows that \(P_n=\frac{1}{2}\) for all \(n\ge 2\).

That's perhaps a slightly unexpected result. It makes me wonder whether I can find an intuitive way of understanding why this should be so, not a complicated analytical way.

To start with, notice that, if \(n=16\), then \(P(T_{16}=15)=0\), because \(T_{m}= 15\) where \(m\le 15\). And similarly, passenger \(n\) can only ever end up in seat \(1\) or \(n\).

Can we perhaps see why these two outcomes should be equally likely?

There are repeated occasions when a passenger makes a choice to occupy a seat that is not their assigned seat. If seat \(1\) and seat \(n\) are both free, then the probabilities that the passenger chooses one or the other or these are equal. Once one of them has been chosen, the other must be the final destination of passenger \(n\).
